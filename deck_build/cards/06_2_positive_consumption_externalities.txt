# 6.2 Positive consumption externalities, evaluation of policies, externality summary (book p177-184)
# Excluded: HL welfare loss calculation (p179); supplementary area algebra (p179).

@ T6.2.1 | definition example | 177
Q: What are positive consumption externalities? Give an example.
A: External benefits created by consumers, e.g. education benefits the person educated and also society: a more productive workforce, lower unemployment, faster growth, more development and lower crime.

@ T6.2.1 | example | 177
Q: Why does the consumption of health care create positive consumption externalities?
A: A healthier population is more productive, enjoys a higher standard of living, passes on fewer contagious diseases and may achieve faster economic growth.

@ T6.2.1 | diagram | 177-178
Q: Describe a positive consumption externality diagram.
A: S = MPC = MSC slopes up; D = MPB slopes down; MSB lies above MPB (the gap is the external benefit). The market produces Qm where MPB = MPC; the optimum Qopt is where MSB = MSC, with Qopt greater than Qm.

@ T6.2.1 | chain | 178
Q: Why does a positive consumption externality lead to an underallocation of resources?
A: Consumers consider only their private benefits → demand reflects MPB, not MSB → Qm is less than Qopt → too little is produced and consumed; at Qm, MSB exceeds MSC.

@ T6.2.1 | explain | 178
Q: What do all positive externalities, whether from production or consumption, have in common?
A: They lead to an underallocation of resources to the good and to its underprovision.

@ T6.2.2 | diagram | 178
Q: Where is the welfare loss on a positive consumption externality diagram?
A: The triangle between MSB and MSC (the supply curve) over the underproduced units from Qm to Qopt, with its point at Qopt.

@ T6.2.2 | diagram | 179
Q: In which direction does the welfare loss triangle point for negative externalities, and for positive externalities?
A: <b>Negative externalities</b> (Qopt less than Qm): it points left, towards Qopt.<br><b>Positive externalities</b> (Qopt greater than Qm): it points right, towards Qopt.

@ T6.2.3 | definition | 179
Q: In market failure terms, what are merit goods?
A: Goods (and services) held to be desirable for consumers, but which are underprovided by the market.

@ T6.2.3 | explain example | 179
Q: How do positive externalities cause the underprovision of merit goods? Give examples.
A: Private benefits are less than social benefits, so the market provides too little, e.g. education and immunisation programs (which also protect the wider population by wiping out a disease).

@ T6.2.3 | explain example | 179
Q: How do low incomes and poverty cause the underprovision of merit goods?
A: People on low incomes may be willing but not able to buy goods such as health care, medicines, education and recreational facilities, so their desire does not show up as demand and market demand is too low.

@ T6.2.3 | explain example | 179
Q: How does consumer ignorance cause the underprovision of merit goods?
A: Consumers may not know about the benefits, so they demand too little, e.g. preventive health care such as immunisation and annual check-ups.

@ T6.2.3 | explain | 179
Q: Can more than one cause of underprovision affect a merit good at the same time?
A: Yes, e.g. health care may be underprovided because of positive externalities, low incomes and consumer ignorance all at once.

@ T6.2.4 | diagram example | 179
Q: Diagram: how does legislation such as compulsory education correct a positive consumption externality?
A: It increases demand: D1 = MPB shifts right towards MSB, ideally until D2 = MSB, so Qopt is produced and consumed and the price rises to Popt.

@ T6.2.4 | explain example | 179-180
Q: How can education and awareness creation correct a positive consumption externality?
A: By persuading consumers to buy more of goods with external benefits (e.g. encouraging the use of sports facilities for health): demand shifts right towards MSB, reaching Qopt, with price rising to Popt.

@ T6.2.4 | explain example | 180
Q: How can nudges correct a positive consumption externality? Give an example.
A: Like education, they increase demand towards MSB, e.g. creating bicycle lanes encourages cycling for exercise (and also reduces the negative externality from petrol use in cars).

@ T6.2.5 | example | 180
Q: What are the most important examples of direct government provision of goods with positive consumption externalities, and why are they provided?
A: Public education and health care in virtually all countries: these merit goods have external benefits so large that it is widely believed they must not be left to private provision alone (though private provision usually exists alongside).

@ T6.2.5 | diagram | 180
Q: Diagram: how does direct government provision correct a positive consumption externality?
A: Supply increases: S shifts right (down) to 'S + government provision', intersecting MPB at Qopt; price falls to Pc and allocative efficiency is achieved.

@ T6.2.5 | diagram | 180
Q: Diagram: how does a subsidy to producers correct a positive consumption externality?
A: A subsidy equal to the external benefit shifts supply right (down) to 'MPC minus subsidy', which intersects MPB at Qopt; price falls from Pm to Pc and allocative efficiency is achieved.

@ T6.2.5 | distinguish | 180
Q: Correcting a positive consumption externality: how does the price paid by consumers differ between demand-side and supply-side policies?
A: <b>Demand-side</b> (legislation, education, nudges): demand rises, so price rises.<br><b>Supply-side</b> (direct provision, subsidies): supply rises, so price falls.<br>Both can bring output to Qopt.

@ T6.2.5 | explain | 180-181
Q: Which government methods can address the underprovision of merit goods?
A: Legislation, education and awareness creation, direct government provision and subsidies: all aim to increase the quantity produced and consumed.

@ T6.2.6 | evaluation | 181
Q: What are the strengths of direct government provision and subsidies for correcting positive externalities?
A: They are very effective at increasing the quantity produced and consumed, and they also lower the price to consumers. They are widely used for consumption externalities and, to a lesser extent, production externalities.

@ T6.2.6 | evaluation | 181
Q: Why do direct provision and subsidies force governments to make difficult choices?
A: They are funded from tax revenue, which has many alternative uses (opportunity costs); as not every good with positive externalities can be supported, governments must choose which goods to support and by how much.

@ T6.2.6 | evaluation | 181
Q: Ideally, how should governments choose which goods to provide or subsidise, and why is this hard in practice?
A: On economic criteria: the social benefits expected relative to their cost, maximising benefits for a given cost. In practice external benefits are very hard to measure, so the right goods and level of support are hard to calculate.

@ T6.2.6 | evaluation | 181
Q: How can politics distort direct provision and subsidies?
A: Different groups compete over who receives the most benefits, and governments under political pressure may choose on political rather than economic criteria.

@ T6.2.6 | evaluation | 181
Q: Realistically, what can policies to correct positive externalities achieve?
A: Governments are very unlikely to shift MPC or MPB by exactly the amount needed; the most that can be hoped for is a step in the right direction.

@ T6.2.6 | evaluation | 181
Q: How effective are legislation and education on their own in correcting positive consumption externalities?
A: Only sometimes: they work well in certain cases (compulsory schooling, education about good nutrition), but cannot on their own raise consumption of health care and education to the optimum.

@ T6.2.6 | evaluation | 181
Q: Why are legislation and awareness creation often combined with direct provision or subsidies? Give an example.
A: Demand-side policies raise the price, which may make the good unaffordable for some groups, so they work better alongside supply-side support, e.g. compulsory schooling (legislation) combined with government-provided schools.

@ T6.2.6 | definition | 182
Q: What are conditional cash transfers (CCTs)?
A: Cash payments to poor families made on conditions, such as sending their children to school and getting them vaccinated.

@ T6.2.6 | example | 182
Q: What is Brazil's Bolsa Familia program, and what positive externality did it create?
A: A conditional cash transfer program that reduced poverty in the short term and raised human capital in the long term. Studies found it also reduced economically motivated crime (mainly robberies) in São Paulo, because of reduced poverty and inequality.

@ T6.2.7 | diagram | 184
Q: On a diagram, how do production externalities differ from consumption externalities?
A: <b>Production externalities:</b> the supply curve splits in two (MPC and MSC differ).<br><b>Consumption externalities:</b> the demand curve splits in two (MPB and MSB differ).

@ T6.2.7 | explain | 184
Q: At the market's point of production, how do MSC and MSB compare for negative and for positive externalities?
A: <b>Negative</b> (external costs): MSC exceeds MSB.<br><b>Positive</b> (external benefits): MSB exceeds MSC.

@ T6.2.7 | diagram | 184
Q: Rule for drawing externality diagrams: is Qm larger or smaller than Qopt?
A: <b>Negative externality:</b> Qm is larger than Qopt (the market provides too much of a bad thing).<br><b>Positive externality:</b> Qm is smaller than Qopt (too little of a good thing).

@ T6.2.7 | diagram | 184
Q: Rule for drawing externality diagrams: which curves determine Qm, and which determine Qopt?
A: Qm is where the private curves meet (MPB and MPC); Qopt is where the social curves meet (MSB and MSC). The ordinary demand and supply curves are the private ones: D = MPB, S = MPC.

@ T6.2.7 | diagram | 184
Q: Rule for drawing externality diagrams: how do you locate the welfare loss?
A: It is the triangle lying between the two curves that have split, pointing towards Qopt.

@ T6.2.7 | diagram | 184
Q: Step by step, how can you label a production externality diagram without memorising it?
A: Draw D and two parallel upward-sloping curves; find the two quantities where they cross D. For a negative externality label the larger Qm and the smaller Qopt (reverse for positive). The curve through Qm is MPC, the one through Qopt is MSC. Shade the triangle between them pointing to Qopt.

@ T6.2.7 | explain | 183
Q: Which market-based policies are used to correct negative production externalities?
A: Indirect (Pigouvian) taxes, carbon taxes and tradable permits.

@ T6.2.7 | explain | 183
Q: Besides market-based policies, which responses are used against negative production externalities and the overuse of common pool resources?
A: Legislation and regulation, collective self-governance, education and awareness creation, and international agreements.

@ T6.2.7 | explain | 183
Q: Which policies are used to correct negative consumption externalities?
A: Indirect (Pigouvian) taxes, legislation and regulation, education and awareness creation, and nudges.

@ T6.2.7 | explain | 183
Q: Which policies are used to correct positive production externalities?
A: Direct government provision and subsidies.

@ T6.2.7 | explain | 183
Q: Which policies are used to correct positive consumption externalities?
A: Legislation and regulation, education and awareness creation, nudges, direct government provision and subsidies.
